Questions are rarely just requests for information. The more ambiguous a question is, the more it forces another choice: do you answer from your own understanding, or first work out what the other person expects to hear?

Every answer sits somewhere between those two positions. At one end, you assert your own view so completely that the answer becomes narcissistic. At the other, you tailor yourself to the questioner's expectations until the answer becomes pandering.

Answering from your own perspective preserves something important: identity. You are not merely producing the answer that fits the room; you are offering how the subject looks from where you stand. But if you refuse to consider why the question is being asked, that identity can harden into self-importance. You may give a true answer that is useless to the person asking.

Tailoring an answer offers the inverse benefit. It helps you understand what the other person actually needs and makes your answer more relevant. But if their expectation determines the whole answer, you compromise your own ideas. The answer becomes less a meeting of perspectives and more a performance of what you believe they want to hear.

Clarification lets you move between these positions without surrendering to either. By probing further with questions like “What do you mean?”, “Why are you asking?”, and “Which part matters?”, you reduce the ambiguity. You are not trying to reverse-engineer the acceptable answer. You are finding the question beneath the question.

Once the question is clearer, you can answer in your own voice while making the answer useful to the other person. Perhaps a good answer does not occupy a fixed point on the spectrum. It moves: accommodating the purpose of the question without abandoning the identity of the person answering.
